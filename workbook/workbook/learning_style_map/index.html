Learning dimensions

Learning style is more than input format

This map looks at several dimensions of learning. It is not a fixed label; it is a way to notice what makes learning clearer, more available, and easier to use.

Input format

The format information arrives in can change how easy it is to understand and remember.

Visual, Auditory, Reading / writing, Kinesthetic, Tactile, Taste, SmellInput can include language, images, movement, touch, taste, smell, and combinations of several channels.Open subtypes
Visual

Learns more easily through images, diagrams, color, layout, icons, maps, and spatial organization.

Auditory

Benefits from spoken explanation, discussion, rhythm, listening, or saying ideas out loud.

Reading / writing

Uses written words, notes, lists, captions, labels, and rewriting to organize understanding.

Kinesthetic

Learns through body movement, acting things out, practicing actions, gestures, or physically doing the process.

Tactile

Uses touch, texture, pressure, manipulatives, materials, or physical objects to make ideas more concrete.

Taste

May connect learning to flavor, food-based examples, oral sensory experience, or taste-related memory cues.

Smell

May notice scent strongly; familiar or low-scent environments can affect focus, memory, comfort, or access.

Multisensory

Benefits when several channels work together, such as seeing, hearing, touching, moving, and naming at the same time.

Processing style

Processing style describes how information is organized, paced, connected, and turned into understanding.

Reflective, Fast-paced, Sequential, Big-picture, Detail-oriented, Pattern-basedThis dimension is about the route thinking takes, not whether someone is quick, slow, good, or bad at learning.Open subtypes
Reflective

Understanding develops through pauses, internal thinking, drafts, and time to form a response.

Fast-paced

Thinking feels more available when there is momentum, challenge, and quick movement between ideas.

Sequential

Information makes sense through ordered steps, clear progression, and cause-and-effect links.

Big-picture / conceptual

The overall purpose, theme, or concept needs to be visible before details feel meaningful.

Detail-oriented

Small facts, precision, exceptions, and exact wording are important parts of understanding.

Pattern-based

Learning happens through noticing similarities, systems, categories, rules, or repeated structures.

Associative

Ideas connect through memory, emotion, examples, side links, and non-linear relationships.

Social setting

Social setting describes how the presence, role, and number of other people shape learning.

Independent, One-to-one, Small group, Group-based, ParallelThis dimension includes privacy, audience, feedback, collaboration, and whether other people add energy or demand.Open subtypes
Independent

Learning happens mostly through private processing, self-direction, and reduced social interruption.

One-to-one

Learning is shaped by direct exchange with one trusted person, mentor, teacher, or peer.

Small group

A few familiar people create room for discussion without the intensity of a large audience.

Group-based

Shared examples, multiple perspectives, social energy, and collaboration are part of the learning process.

Parallel

Another person nearby may affect focus even when people are working on separate tasks.

Audience-sensitive

Performance, confidence, or language access changes depending on who is watching or listening.

Engagement needs

Engagement needs describe what makes attention, motivation, and participation easier to access.

Interactive, Interest-led, Movement-supported, Novelty, Challenge, AutonomyThis dimension is about what pulls the learner into the material and what makes the learning feel alive enough to stay with.Open subtypes
Interactive

Attention is supported by participation, questions, feedback, experiments, and active exchange.

Interest-led

Motivation is strongest when the topic connects to curiosity, fascination, or a deep interest.

Movement-supported

Engagement is connected to body movement, gestures, fidgets, standing, walking, or physical action.

Novelty-seeking

Newness, variety, surprise, or changing formats can make attention easier to sustain.

Challenge-based

A clear puzzle, goal, problem, or level of difficulty can create focus and momentum.

Creative / expressive

Making, designing, storytelling, humor, metaphor, or personal expression helps the material matter.

Autonomy-supported

Engagement is shaped by having meaningful choice, control over approach, or ownership of the task.

Support needs

Support needs describe the scaffolds that reduce confusion, uncertainty, memory load, or pressure.

Clear structure, Examples, Repetition, Time to prepare, Chunking, FeedbackThis dimension names the kind of scaffolding a learner may need; it is not a measure of ability.Open subtypes
Clear structure

Understanding depends on explicit steps, expectations, boundaries, and what counts as complete.

Examples

Models, samples, demonstrations, or comparison cases make abstract expectations more concrete.

Repetition

Repeated exposure, reminders, and revisiting information are part of how learning settles.

Time to prepare

Preview, planning, and reduced surprise affect readiness to participate or produce work.

Chunking

Information is easier to hold when it is divided into smaller parts, stages, or checkpoints.

Feedback

Response from another person, the task, or the environment helps calibrate what is understood.

Memory scaffolding

External cues, labels, lists, prompts, or reminders reduce the load on working memory.

After learning

Reflection time

Before taking a test, pause with your own experience. The goal is not to find one perfect label; it is to notice what feels familiar, what feels new, and what you may want to try.

Take-away

What I can take from this

Input format

Input can include language, images, movement, touch, taste, smell, and combinations of several channels.

  • Ask for information in more than one format when possible.
  • Notice whether visual, auditory, reading / writing, or hands-on input helps most for this task.

Processing style

This dimension is about the route thinking takes, not whether someone is quick, slow, good, or bad at learning.

  • Name the pace and structure that help: reflective, fast-paced, sequential, or big-picture.
  • Ask for preview time, a concept map, or step-by-step order when the default pace does not fit.

Social setting

This dimension includes privacy, audience, feedback, collaboration, and whether other people add energy or demand.

  • Choose the social setting intentionally: independent, one-to-one, or group-based.
  • Make group learning clearer with roles, turn-taking, and a way to ask questions safely.

Engagement needs

This dimension is about what pulls the learner into the material and what makes the learning feel alive enough to stay with.

  • Use interaction, interest, choice, and movement to make attention easier to access.
  • Connect new material to something meaningful before expecting sustained focus.

Support needs

This dimension names the kind of scaffolding a learner may need; it is not a measure of ability.

  • Use clear structure, examples, repetition, and time to prepare as legitimate learning supports.
  • Keep reusable scaffolds such as checklists, models, worked examples, and practice rounds.
