Academic writing · 05

Editing without getting stuck.

Sometimes I think the writing needs to change. Sometimes someone else does.

Back to academic writing

In this article

01 · Two starting points

Editing can begin in two different ways.

Sometimes I read my own writing and feel that it is not working.

Sometimes another person reads it and tells me that something is not working.

These situations feel different. They also need different responses.

02 · Edit for myself

When I can see the problem, I try hard to solve it.

I rewrite sentences. I move paragraphs. I remove detail. I make the main point easier to see.

I may turn the structure into a flowchart. I may make a table that connects each claim to its evidence. A visual view can reveal repetition, gaps, and weak links.

I continue until the writing feels satisfying to me.

Self-editing still has an upper limit. I am working with my own vocabulary, habits, and standards.

After a certain point, another round may take me around the same circle. That is when I need to stop or show the work to someone else.

03 · Experienced feedback

A conversation can teach more than a correction.

Some feedback comes from a person with many years of writing experience.

I try to talk with them when I can.

What did they notice? Why is the new version more effective? What would help the reader understand the point sooner?

The reason behind an edit can be useful long after that sentence is gone.

04 · Vague feedback

An unclear comment can still point towards a problem.

Sometimes feedback is vague. Sometimes I have the feeling that the person may not have followed the whole paper.

The comment may still be a signal that something important was difficult to see.

I can take it to a more experienced writer. I can also ask AI to suggest several possible interpretations.

Is this a style problem? Is the emphasis hidden? Is a logical step missing? Or is the comment itself too ambiguous to guide a change?

I can then respond according to the situation. I do not need to treat every vague comment in the same way.

05 · Reviewer and editor requests

I choose where to spend my energy.

If a reviewer or editor suggests a change that serves the paper and does not distort the work, it is often simplest to make it.

I say this because I sometimes resist changing something that already feels good to me.

One small edit can affect the sentences around it. The thought of that chain reaction can feel stressful. I may delay opening the document.

The change is often easier once I begin.

A suggestion is not a rejection of my ability to write. Learning how to receive, interpret, and respond to suggestions is part of becoming a writer.

06 · Build a practice

I want to make editing more fun.

A paper may pass through many rounds of editing.

Some changes will feel meaningful. Others may feel less important.

Over time, I can build my own editing process and my own way of thinking about revision.

I try not to keep editing when I am becoming deeply irritated. When that happens, I go and do something else.

I return after the feeling has settled. Looking back, editing can be quite enjoyable. It can also be much easier than it felt in that moment.

When editing becomes less painful, writing becomes easier to continue. I can write more. I can also give other people feedback that is more specific, accurate, and useful.
