Understand your relationship with money.

Money can influence a career decision before we have named what it means to us.

01 · Money and science

Many people leave science partly for financial reasons.

You may be certain that science is your lifelong subject. You may still be deciding.

Either way, I hope you will spend some time thinking about your relationship with money.

A career decision can look as if it is only about interest or ability. Money may be making part of the decision quietly.

02 · What money means

What does money represent to you?

Security

If money means security, a satisfying amount in your bank account may matter greatly whenever life feels uncertain. It is reasonable to give that goal a high priority. If the amount is within reach, you may choose to earn and save it before returning to academia. Once the security is there, you can ask what you want next.

Freedom

If money means freedom, savings may be one route. You can also collect forms of freedom that require little money. Time, useful skills, supportive relationships, and a life with fewer fixed costs may all create room to choose.

There are many other meanings. Money may represent care, status, possibility, responsibility, power, generosity, or rest.

You do not need to act immediately. You can begin by noticing what you feel.

03 · Learn about money

Financial knowledge includes psychology.

A book worth reading

The Psychology of Money by Morgan Housel is a useful place to begin.

Housel offers a striking estimate. Personal experience may represent only 0.00000001% of what has happened in the world, yet account for perhaps 80% of how someone thinks the world works.

The book discusses research suggesting that investment decisions are strongly anchored to the market returns people experienced early in adult life.

Growing up around a booming market can make investing in shares feel natural. Living through a depression or repeated crashes can make the same market feel unsafe for many years.

Financial choices may therefore have less to do with objective facts than we imagine.

Learning about money creates more room to choose. How would you like to be friends with money? How would you like to store it, spend it, share it, or invest it?

04 · A spending exercise

How much money can you imagine using?

Take a notebook. Each day, imagine receiving twice as much money as the day before. The full amount must be used that day.

Begin the doubling

  • Day 1Spend £1,000
  • Day 2Spend £2,000
  • Day 3Spend £4,000
  • Day 4Spend £8,000
  • Keep goingDouble the amount again each day.
  • Day 20Spend £524,288,000

How many days can you continue before you run out of ideas?

The exercise explores more than desire. It also explores your sense of large numbers and the life you can imagine living with that amount of wealth.

Some people stop at one number. Some continue. Either response may reveal something worth noticing.

You can keep playing for as long as you like. Day 20 is only a marker, not the end of the game.

05 · A material world

Understanding money can become another way to understand yourself.

We live in a material society.

Let’s face it. Unless you grow, gather, or hunt all your own food, you rely on money and exchange to eat.

Food reaches us through farmers, processors, transport, shops, and many other hands. Behind that journey is a complex supply chain, shaped at every stage by financial decisions.

Knowing your relationship with money can clarify what you want to create, what you want to leave behind, what you want to consume, and who you want to become.
